Scaling Guide — the Secondary scaler and Output Upscale

ChitraMaya scales pixels in two very different places, and it pays to keep them apart in your head:

Secondary (Restoration panel) Output Upscale (Encoder panel)
What it scales each restored region (a 256-px crop), before paste-back the whole frame, after restoration, before encode
Why large regions were stretched at paste-back and went soft you want a bigger output (720p → 2160p, 1080p → 1440p / 2160p)
Cost a few percent of a run hours per hour of video on most cards
Default on (RTX Super-Res 4× on NVIDIA, Real-ESRGAN 4× elsewhere) off
Use it for every run clips and segments; titles overnight, and only on NVIDIA's RTX path in reasonable time

Both take the same three kinds of scaler: RTX Super-Res (NVIDIA Maxine; needs an RTX card and a recent driver), Real-ESRGAN 4× (bundled, every edition), and custom models — any super-resolution .pth / .safetensors you drop into models\upscalers\.

1. The Secondary — why it is on by default

The restorer works on a fixed 256 × 256 crop of each region. When the region on the frame is larger than that — close-ups, VR, anything at 4K — the restored crop used to be stretched back up at paste-back, and the result went soft exactly where you look closest. With a secondary scaler the restored crop is upscaled 4× first (to 1024 px), so the final resize shrinks instead of stretching. Regions at or below 256 px skip the stage automatically (the run report's counts.secondary says how many crops went through and how many were too small to need it).

Cost is small because only regions are scaled: on an RTX 4060 the 17 s 1080p test cut restores in 32.5 s with RTX Super-Res 4× and 32.5 s with Real-ESRGAN 4× — the same. On AMD and Intel the secondary runs in PyTorch and is a larger share of a slower run, but still single-digit percent.

Which scaler? They produce two different looks, and neither is more correct — pick by eye with Test Frame, which shows every region as Mosaic → Restored:

  • RTX Super-Res (NVIDIA): the smoother result — about half the fine texture of the other path on the same clip, with the same residual mosaic and flicker. Its Scaler Quality (low / medium / high / ultra; high is the pre-1.80 value) and Scaler Denoise (a second Maxine pass) are the two controls beneath it. Denoise changed nothing visible in our measurements at ~60 % more restore time; leave it at none.
  • Real-ESRGAN 4× (realesr-general-x4v3, every edition): keeps the restorer's texture. The default on AMD and Intel, and a fair choice on NVIDIA if you prefer texture to smoothness.
  • A custom model — see §3. The one we recommend trying is 4xNomos8k_span_otf_strong (a SPAN model trained by Philip Hofmann): to our eyes a touch better than either built-in on skin, and on AMD and Intel it is faster than Real-ESRGAN (9 % less restore time on an RX 9060 XT, 18 % on an Arc 140V) once its layers are fused at load, which ChitraMaya does. On NVIDIA it costs about the same as the built-ins.

If RTX Super-Res is selected on a machine where the Maxine runtime is not available, the run falls back to Real-ESRGAN and the console and run report (effective.secondary) say so.

Region Margin (Detection panel) grows the detector's box before it reaches the restorer and the paste-back mask with it; Feather and Blend Mask (Restoration panel) shape the edge where the scaled crop meets the frame — the Face Swap blend feathers outward into clean skin, so every mosaic pixel is fully replaced.

2. Output Upscale — a clip feature, or an overnight one

Output Upscale (Encoder panel) takes a factor — 2×, 4× — or a target — 1080p, 1440p, 2160p — and scales every finished frame before it is encoded. It works inside a normal restoration run, and on its own in Upscale mode (Alternate Execution Modes): decode → scale → encode, no detector, no restorer, no tracker — for SFW content you simply want bigger, or a restored output you want to bring to 4K in a second pass. Save Segment writes a marked segment as its own file, so you can judge a scaler on a minute before committing a title. The Upscale Scaler dropdown picks the model; the progress modal, the console checkpoints and the run report (counts.output_upscale — scaler, factor, ms per frame) all know the mode.

A 4× model is run once and the result resized to the target when the target is not a multiple of the model's factor (a 4× model to 2160p from 1080p runs at 4× and shrinks by half); a 2× model to 2160p from 720p runs twice. The run report's effective.output_upscale shows what happened.

What it costs — measured

Whole-frame scaling touches every pixel of every frame, so it is a different animal from the Secondary. These are real runs, not estimates:

card job scaler speed
RTX 3060 Ti 8 GB 1080p title restored and upscaled to 2160p RTX Super-Res (Quality ultra, Denoise ultra) 6h30m for 2h03m (0.32×); the upscale was 81 % of the wall; 85 ms per frame
RTX 5060 Ti 1080p title, Upscale mode to 2160p RTX Super-Res (ultra + ultra) 10h02m for 2h41m (0.27×); 61 ms per frame
RTX 3060 Ti 8 GB 1080p title, Upscale mode to 2160p Real-ESRGAN ~29 h for 2h03m (2.1 fps)
RTX 3060 Ti 8 GB 1080p30 title (66 min) restored and upscaled to 2160p RTX Super-Res (ultra + ultra) 3h42m (0.30×); 86.6 ms per frame; VRAM at the 8 GB ceiling from mid-title (no paging)
RTX 3060 Ti 8 GB the same title, same settings custom 4× SPAN with its TensorRT engines 5h04m (0.22×); 131 ms per frame (275 in PyTorch = 2.1×); VRAM 72 %
RX 9060 XT 16 GB 720p title restored and upscaled to 2160p custom 4× SPAN 10h00m for 2h25m (0.24×); 98 ms per frame; the upscale was 71 % of the wall
RX 9060 XT 16 GB 17 s 1080p cut restored and upscaled to 2160p custom 4× SPAN 143 s (3.9 fps); 183 ms per frame for the upscale alone
Arc 140V (laptop iGPU) 17 s 1080p cut restored and upscaled to 2160p custom 4× SPAN 505 s (1.1 fps); the scaler took 76 ms per frame, the 4K frames' trip to the encoder the rest

Read it as three tiers:

  • NVIDIA with RTX Super-Res: the title-scale path. 60–85 ms per 4K frame at the ultra + ultra settings those runs used, i.e. roughly three to four hours per hour of 1080p60 video on a 3060 Ti–5060 Ti class card — and note the Known issue below: those runs paid for a second denoise pass they did not need. At the defaults (Quality high, Denoise none) expect less.
  • Real-ESRGAN and custom models on NVIDIA: clips — or, with engines, a long night. A compact 4× network over a 4K frame is ~475 ms on a 3060 Ti in PyTorch; a 2-hour title is a 29-hour job. A custom model compiled to TensorRT engines (§3) runs 2.1× faster: a SPAN model at 131 ms per 4K frame brought a 66-minute title to five hours on the same card, with 2 GB of VRAM to spare where RTX Super-Res sat at the ceiling. Still half the speed of RTX Super-Res; use it where you prefer its look.
  • AMD and Intel: clips, or overnight. There is no Maxine, so every whole-frame scaler is a PyTorch network over every frame — 100–180 ms per frame on an RX 9060 XT, and on the Arc 140V the 4K output itself runs at about 1 fps because the frames' trip to the encoder is the wall. A 720p title to 2160p on the 9060 XT is a ten-hour job; keep titles at their native resolution on the Arc.

The console's [Pipeline] frame N/M avg X fps ... ETA line prints the true average every 500 frames; a minute into a run you know the whole run's length. Save Segment first, then decide.

The two things to know before a title

  1. Upscale Scaler = RTX Super-Res takes its Quality and Denoise from the Secondary's controls (a known issue in 1.80.00; own controls are planned). With the Secondary at ultra + ultra, every 4K frame gets a second denoise pass. Set the Secondary's Quality / Denoise to what you want the upscale to run at — the defaults, high / none, are the cheap and sensible pair — or accept the cost knowingly.
  2. Resolution and memory. A 2160p output is four times the pixels of 1080p at every stage after the scaler — the encoder's surfaces, the frames in flight. On 8 GB cards, and on 16 GB laptops, the VRAM / RAM preflight in the console is worth reading before a long run.

3. Custom models — models\upscalers\

Drop any super-resolution model file into models\upscalers\ (or use Manage Models → Custom scalers → Add) and press Rescan: it appears in both scaler dropdowns as <file> (<architecture> Nx). ChitraMaya identifies the architecture from the file itself — Real-ESRGAN / ESRGAN / RRDB, SPAN, PLKSR and RealPLKSR, DAT, HAT, OmniSR, SwinIR, ATD (community layout), Compact and some thirty more — through the spandrel library. Files it cannot identify are listed with a reason (official-repository ATD checkpoints, for instance, use a revised layout the identifier does not know yet; a training checkpoint, a diffusion model, or a YOLO file in the wrong folder each get their own hint).

What runs, and how:

  • Custom models run in PyTorch on every edition (fp16 where the GPU supports it), tiled when the frame is large, with the model's required padding handled for it.
  • As a Secondary, a custom model's output is colour-matched to the restorer's crop before paste-back (mean and contrast per channel, gain clamped), so a model's tint never leaks into the frame. Its factor is honoured: a 2× model upscales the crop 2×.
  • As the Upscale Scaler, the model's factor and your target decide the pass count, as in §2.
  • SPAN models get one special treatment: the reference implementation re-fuses their reparameterised layers on every frame, which made them run slower than they are; ChitraMaya fuses them once at load (bit-identical output, checked), which is why a SPAN model is the fastest custom option.
  • NVIDIA: compile the model once (CM-247). From the install folder:

ChitraMaya-cli.exe -compile-sr 4xNomos8k_span_otf_strong.pth --secondary --bench

writes TensorRT engines beside the weights in models\upscalers\ — the whole-frame engine and, with --secondary, the 256-px engine the Secondary uses on crops; --bench prints the speed-up and the parity against PyTorch. From then on the app uses them automatically ([Scalers] ... engine ACTIVE in the console; backend: "tensorrt" in the run report). Measured 2.1× on the whole-frame upscale; the Secondary engine on crops is faster than Maxine's. Engines are built for the GPU they were compiled on — one built on another card is skipped with a console line and the model runs in PyTorch. A few minutes per model; needs a few GB of free VRAM while it builds. AMD and Intel: no engines; the model runs in PyTorch (the fused SPAN path is already the fast one there).

Where to find models: OpenModelDB is the community index; Philip Hofmann's models repository carries many of the best-regarded ones with training notes. Check each model's licence — they differ, and they are not ChitraMaya's to grant.

What to expect: as a Secondary, none of the community models we measured beat the two built-ins clearly; 4xNomos8k_span_otf_strong is the one that came closest — to our eyes slightly better on skin, and faster on AMD and Intel. As a whole-frame scaler the picture is more open, because the built-in Real-ESRGAN is a compact network chosen for speed, and a stronger model may well look better on a clip you care about — at a cost the table above lets you estimate. Measure with Save Segment; trust your eyes over any of our numbers.

4. Max Clip Length — measured

Max Clip Length is the restorer's temporal window: a track is cut into clips of at most that many frames, each restored in one pass with the frames before and after it in view, and the cuts are joined with Overlap + Crossfade. Six runs of a 10-minute 4K60 cut on an RTX 3060 12 GB (identical detections in every run; RTX Super-Res and Real-ESRGAN Secondaries):

Max Clip wall clips join re-work VRAM peak residual scan
90 48:49 1,020 23 % 7.9 GB 26 frames
180 46:28 668 12.5 % 9.8 GB 25 (same frames)
240 45:55 598 10 % 11.1 GB 26 (same frames)
300 54:09 563 9 % 12.2 GB = ceiling, paging 26 (same frames)

The restore cost per frame was flat (29.6 / 29.8 / 30.0 ms at 90 / 180 / 240); the only thing a short clip costs is the join re-work, and the only thing a long one buys is fewer joins — until it hits the card's memory, when everything slows, detection included (300: +18 % wall). The scaler made no difference to any of this (Real-ESRGAN: 55:23 / 50:30 at 90 / 180).

Rule: Max Clip Length ≈ 3 × frame rate — 180 at 60 fps, 90 at 30 — the 1.80.00 default. Going shorter buys VRAM (about 2 GB between 180 and 90 at 4K) for a few percent of time and more joins; going longer buys nothing you can see. On an 8 GB card at 4K60, 90 fits and 180 does not without Crop Cap; take Max Clip 90 before Crop Cap 512, because it costs no texture.

5. Recipes

A 1080p title, best quality in reasonable time (NVIDIA): defaults (Secondary RTX Super-Res 4×, Temporal Stability 1, Output Upscale off). Try Temporal Stability 3 on a segment if shimmer bothers you.

The same title on AMD or Intel: defaults (Secondary Real-ESRGAN 4×), or 4xNomos8k_span_otf_strong as the Secondary for a slightly better and faster run. Output Upscale off.

A 720p or 1080p title you want at 2160p (NVIDIA): Output Upscale 2160p with Upscale Scaler RTX Super-Res, Secondary Quality high, Denoise none. Overnight on a 3060 Ti-class card; check the ETA line after the first 500 frames.

A clip or segment you want at 2160p with a particular look (any edition): mark the segment, Output Upscale 2160p with Real-ESRGAN or a custom model, Save Segment. Minutes, not hours.

SFW content you just want bigger: Alternate Execution Modes → Upscale, pick the target and scaler, Restore & Save. No models to download.

A 4K VR title on an 8 GB card: the built-in VR 4K configuration (Image Size 800, Max Clip 180, Split SBS), Secondary on, Output Upscale off, and Crop Cap if the preflight asks for it.
